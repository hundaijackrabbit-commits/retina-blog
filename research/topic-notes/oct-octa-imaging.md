# OCT and OCTA Imaging Research Notes

## Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) - Established Technology

### Overview
- **Technology**: Uses low-coherence interferometry to create cross-sectional images of retinal tissue
- **Resolution**: Typically 3-7 μm axial resolution in modern spectral-domain OCT
- **Clinical Status**: Established standard of care in ophthalmology since early 2000s
- **Evidence Level**: Established - extensive clinical validation across multiple conditions

### Key Technical Evolution
1. **Time-Domain OCT** (TD-OCT): First generation, slower acquisition
2. **Spectral-Domain OCT** (SD-OCT): Faster, higher resolution, became clinical standard ~2006
3. **Swept-Source OCT** (SS-OCT): Longer wavelength, better penetration, improved visualization of choroid

### Clinical Applications (Established Evidence)
- **Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD)**: Detection and monitoring of drusen, fluid, subretinal lesions
- **Diabetic Macular Edema (DME)**: Quantification of retinal thickness, fluid assessment
- **Glaucoma**: Retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) and ganglion cell complex (GCC) thickness measurement
- **Macular Hole**: Diagnosis and surgical monitoring
- **Central Serous Chorioretinopathy (CSC)**: Subretinal fluid detection
- **Inherited Retinal Disease**: Structure assessment and disease monitoring

### Key Metrics
- **Central Retinal Thickness (CRT)**: Important for macular edema assessment
- **RNFL Thickness**: Glaucoma monitoring
- **Choroidal Thickness**: AMD, CSC, other posterior segment diseases

### Limitations
- **Motion artifacts**: Patient movement can degrade image quality
- **Media opacity**: Cataracts, vitreous hemorrhage reduce signal quality
- **Interpretation variability**: Requires trained interpretation
- **Segmentation errors**: Automated layer segmentation can fail in pathological tissue

## Optical Coherence Tomography Angiography (OCTA)

### Overview
- **Technology**: Motion-contrast based imaging of retinal and choroidal vasculature
- **Principle**: Detects blood flow by comparing sequential OCT B-scans at same location
- **Key Advantage**: Non-invasive vascular imaging without dye injection
- **Clinical Status**: FDA-approved devices available, growing clinical adoption since ~2014
- **Evidence Level**: Supported - strong evidence in specific applications, ongoing validation

### Technical Principles
- **Motion Contrast**: Flowing blood creates signal variance between repeated B-scans
- **vs. Fluorescein Angiography (FA)**: OCTA is faster, no dye, depth-resolved, but smaller field of view
- **vs. Indocyanine Green (ICG)**: OCTA more accessible, non-invasive, but FA/ICG still gold standard for some applications

### Clinical Applications
1. **Diabetic Retinopathy**: 
   - Capillary dropout visualization
   - Foveal avascular zone (FAZ) enlargement
   - Microaneurysm detection
   - Supporting evidence but not replacement for traditional imaging

2. **Age-Related Macular Degeneration**:
   - Choroidal neovascularization (CNV) detection and classification
   - Type 1 vs Type 2 CNV differentiation
   - Treatment response monitoring
   - Strong supporting evidence

3. **Retinal Vein Occlusion (RVO)**:
   - Ischemia assessment
   - Macular perfusion evaluation
   - Emerging evidence

4. **Glaucoma**:
   - Peripapillary and macular vessel density
   - Correlation with functional loss
   - Emerging biomarker potential

### OCTA Artifacts and Limitations
- **Projection artifacts**: Superficial vessels cast shadows onto deeper layers
- **Motion artifacts**: Eye movement creates decorrelation noise
- **Signal strength dependency**: Poor signal degrades vessel visualization
- **Field of view**: Typically 3x3mm or 6x6mm, smaller than FA/ICG
- **No leakage information**: Unlike FA, OCTA cannot show vascular leakage
- **Quantification challenges**: Vessel density metrics not yet fully standardized

### Quantitative Metrics
- **Vessel Density**: Percentage of area occupied by perfused vasculature
- **FAZ Area**: Foveal avascular zone size
- **Perfusion Density**: Blood flow in specific retinal layers
- **Fractal Dimension**: Vascular complexity measure

### Evidence Gaps
- Standardization across devices (different algorithms, flow thresholds)
- Long-term outcome correlation
- Cost-effectiveness vs. traditional angiography
- Optimal integration into clinical workflow

## Ultra-Widefield Imaging

### Overview
- **Field of View**: Up to 200° of retina (vs. ~30-50° traditional fundus photography)
- **Key Devices**: Optos ultra-widefield systems, others
- **Clinical Status**: Supported - valuable for specific indications
- **Evidence Level**: Supported - demonstrated utility in peripheral retinal pathology

### Clinical Applications
- **Diabetic Retinopathy**: Peripheral lesions may have prognostic significance
- **Retinal Detachment**: Better peripheral documentation
- **Retinal Vascular Occlusions**: More complete ischemia assessment  
- **Uveitis**: Peripheral vasculitis detection

### Limitations
- **Distortion**: Peripheral retina appears compressed
- **Eyelash artifacts**: Common in peripheral zones
- **Image quality variation**: Resolution may be lower peripherally than in traditional imaging
- **Not always superior**: Central pole imaging may be better with conventional photography

### Evidence Questions
- Clinical significance of peripheral-only findings
- Cost-effectiveness for screening vs. standard photography
- Optimal integration with OCT/OCTA multimodal imaging
