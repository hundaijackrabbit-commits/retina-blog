# Retinal Diseases Research Notes

## Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD)

### Overview
- **Leading cause** of vision loss in older adults in developed countries
- **Two forms**: Dry (geographic atrophy) and Wet (neovascular)
- **Evidence Level**: Established - extensive research, multiple treatments for wet AMD

### Dry AMD and Geographic Atrophy (GA)
- **Progression**: Drusen → intermediate AMD → advanced dry AMD (GA)
- **GA**: Progressive loss of photoreceptors, RPE, and choriocapillaris
- **Treatment landscape**: Historically no approved treatments; recent developments in complement inhibition
- **Imaging**: Fundus autofluorescence (FAF), OCT, multicolor imaging for monitoring
- **Measurement**: GA area, growth rate (linear vs square root models)

### Neovascular/Wet AMD
- **Pathophysiology**: Choroidal neovascularization (CNV), VEGF-driven
- **Treatment**: Anti-VEGF therapy (ranibizumab, aflibercept, brolucizumab, others)
- **Evidence**: Strong evidence from multiple RCTs (MARINA, ANCHOR, CATT, others)
- **Treatment burden**: Frequent injections, monitoring
- **Treat-and-extend protocols**: Evidence-based approach to reduce burden while maintaining outcomes

### CNV Types
- **Type 1**: Sub-RPE CNV
- **Type 2**: Sub-retinal CNV
- **Type 3**: Retinal angiomatous proliferation (RAP)
- **Clinical relevance**: Different types may have different prognosis, response

### AMD Risk Factors
- **Age**: Strongest risk factor
- **Genetics**: CFH, ARMS2/HTRA1, other complement pathway genes
- **Smoking**: Strong modifiable risk factor
- **Diet**: AREDS formulation (vitamins C, E, zinc, copper, lutein, zeaxanthin)
- **Cardiovascular factors**: Hypertension, others

## Diabetic Retinopathy (DR)

### Overview
- **Leading cause** of vision loss in working-age adults globally
- **Prevalence**: Increases with diabetes duration, poor glycemic control
- **Evidence Level**: Established - extensive natural history studies, RCTs for treatment

### Classification
- **No DR**: No retinal changes
- **Mild NPDR**: Microaneurysms only
- **Moderate NPDR**: More than microaneurysms, less than severe
- **Severe NPDR**: 4-2-1 rule (hemorrhages in 4 quadrants, venous beading in 2+ quadrants, IRMA in 1+ quadrant)
- **Proliferative DR (PDR)**: Neovascularization

### Diabetic Macular Edema (DME)
- **Most common cause** of vision loss in diabetic patients
- **Pathophysiology**: Breakdown of blood-retinal barrier, vascular leakage, VEGF
- **Treatment**: Anti-VEGF (first-line for center-involving DME), corticosteroids (some cases), laser (less common now)
- **Evidence**: Strong from multiple RCTs (DRCR.net protocols, others)

### Imaging in DR
- **Fundus photography**: Standard for classification, screening
- **OCT**: Essential for DME diagnosis, treatment monitoring
- **OCTA**: Emerging for ischemia, microvasculature assessment
- **Ultra-widefield**: Peripheral lesions may predict progression

### Risk Factors and Progression
- **Glycemic control**: HbA1c critical (DCCT, UKPDS studies)
- **Blood pressure**: Control important
- **Pregnancy**: Can accelerate DR progression
- **Duration of diabetes**: Linear increase in risk

## Inherited Retinal Diseases (IRDs)

### Overview
- **Diverse group**: >270 genes implicated
- **Prevalence**: Relatively rare individually, ~1 in 2000-3000 collectively
- **Evidence Level for gene therapy**: Supported for specific indications, Emerging for most

### Major Categories
1. **Retinitis Pigmentosa (RP)**: Progressive rod-cone dystrophy, ~1 in 4000
2. **Stargardt Disease**: Macular dystrophy, ABCA4 gene most common
3. **Leber Congenital Amaurosis (LCA)**: Severe early-onset, multiple genes
4. **Choroideremia**: X-linked, progressive degeneration
5. **Achromatopsia**: Cone dysfunction, day blindness
6. **Usher Syndrome**: Combined retinal + hearing loss

### Diagnosis
- **Clinical exam**: Fundus appearance, visual fields
- **Electrophysiology**: ERG (full-field, multifocal)
- **Imaging**: OCT (photoreceptor layer analysis), FAF (RPE function)
- **Genetic testing**: Essential for diagnosis, genetic counseling, treatment eligibility

### Natural History
- Highly variable by gene and mutation
- Progressive vision loss typical
- Some genes have specific phenotypes (early vs. late onset, central vs. peripheral)

## Retinal Detachment (RD)

### Types
1. **Rhegmatogenous RD (RRD)**: Retinal break leads to subretinal fluid accumulation
2. **Tractional RD**: Fibrovascular tissue pulls retina (e.g., PDR, trauma)
3. **Exudative RD**: Fluid accumulation without break (e.g., tumors, inflammation)

### RRD Risk Factors
- **Myopia**: High myopia significantly increases risk
- **Posterior vitreous detachment (PVD)**: Can create retinal tears
- **Lattice degeneration**: Peripheral retinal thinning
- **Previous RD in fellow eye**
- **Cataract surgery**: Slightly increased risk post-op

### Treatment
- **Surgical**: Vitrectomy, scleral buckle, pneumatic retinopexy
- **Timing**: Often urgent/emergent (macula-involving RD = more urgent)
- **Evidence**: Strong surgical evidence, but RCTs comparing techniques limited

### Prognosis
- **Macula status**: Macula-on vs. macula-off critical for visual outcome
- **Duration**: Chronic RD worse prognosis
- **Proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR)**: Complication that worsens outcome

## Retinal Vein Occlusion (RVO)

### Types
1. **Central RVO (CRVO)**: Occlusion at optic nerve, affects entire retina
2. **Branch RVO (BRVO)**: Occlusion of branch vein, affects sectoral retina

### Pathophysiology
- **Thrombosis** at arteriovenous crossings (BRVO) or lamina cribrosa (CRVO)
- **Consequences**: Hemorrhages, edema, ischemia

### Risk Factors
- **Hypertension**: Most important modifiable factor
- **Cardiovascular disease**
- **Glaucoma**
- **Hypercoagulable states** (less common)

### Treatment
- **Macular edema**: Anti-VEGF, corticosteroids (implant or injection)
- **Evidence**: Strong from RCTs (CRUISE, BRAVO, others)
- **Ischemic CRVO**: May develop neovascular glaucoma; laser photocoagulation in some cases

## Central Serous Chorioretinopathy (CSC)

### Overview
- **Subretinal fluid** accumulation at macula
- **Typical patient**: Young to middle-aged men, often type A personality, stress association
- **Evidence Level**: Established for diagnosis, Emerging for optimal treatment

### Classification
- **Acute CSC**: Usually self-limited, resolves spontaneously
- **Chronic CSC**: Persistent or recurrent, can cause permanent damage

### Pathophysiology
- **Not fully understood**: Choroidal hyperpermeability, RPE dysfunction
- **Corticosteroid association**: Systemic or topical steroids can trigger

### Imaging
- **OCT**: Shows subretinal fluid, may show RPE detachment
- **FA**: Shows focal leak point(s)
- **ICGA**: May show choroidal hyperpermeability
- **OCTA**: Choriocapillaris flow alterations

### Treatment
- **Acute**: Often observation (most resolve spontaneously)
- **Chronic/recurrent**: PDT (photodynamic therapy), laser, anti-VEGF (less evidence), others under investigation
- **Evidence**: PDT has best evidence for chronic CSC, but not FDA-approved for this indication in all countries

## Macular Hole

### Classification
- **Stage 1**: Foveal detachment
- **Stage 2**: Small full-thickness hole (<400 μm)
- **Stage 3**: Large full-thickness hole (>400 μm)
- **Stage 4**: Full-thickness hole with PVD

### Pathophysiology
- **Vitreomacular traction**: Vitreous pulling on fovea
- **Progression**: Not all stage 1 progress to full-thickness

### Treatment
- **Surgical**: Vitrectomy with ILM peeling, gas tamponade
- **Success rate**: High (>90% anatomic closure in primary surgery)
- **Evidence**: Strong surgical evidence

### OCT Role
- **Diagnosis**: OCT is diagnostic standard
- **Prognostic factors**: Minimum diameter, basal diameter, hole index

## Uveitis and Retinal Inflammation

### Posterior Uveitis / Panuveitis
- **Causes**: Infectious (toxoplasmosis, CMV, others), non-infectious (sarcoid, Behçet's, VKH, others), idiopathic
- **Complications**: Macular edema, retinal detachment, optic nerve involvement
- **Imaging**: OCT for edema, FA for vasculitis, ICGA for choroiditis

### Treatment
- **Infectious**: Antimicrobial therapy
- **Non-infectious**: Corticosteroids, immunosuppression, biologics
- **Evidence**: Strong for infectious treatment, Variable for non-infectious (some RCTs, much observational)

## Key Research Directions

1. **Biomarkers for progression**: Which AMD/DR patients will progress rapidly?
2. **Personalized treatment**: Genomics, imaging biomarkers to guide therapy selection
3. **Neuroprotection**: Protecting photoreceptors/RGCs independent of vascular treatment
4. **Long-acting treatments**: Reducing injection burden in AMD, DME
5. **Gene therapy**: Expanding beyond single-gene IRDs
6. **AI for early detection**: Earlier diagnosis, better risk stratification
